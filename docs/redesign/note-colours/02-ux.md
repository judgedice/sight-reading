# Note colours: UX proposal

Role: principal UX. Input: the owner's request ("associate a colour with each note"), `01-research.md`, `docs/redesign/06-design-spec.md` §3 and §7, `docs/redesign/round2/04-design.md`, the README.
Status: proposal only. Nothing in `src/`, `config/` or `design/` changes. Items marked **Owner's choice** need the owner to decide. Everything else is my recommendation, and I would build it as written unless the designer or the owner shows me a reason not to.

## The short version

- **Colour labels the written note, never the verdict.** Noteheads stay ink. The colour sits on a small chip with the note's letter (or solfège syllable) inside it, in a band under each system.
- **Colour is for studying the music, not for playing it.** It shows in the read-through and the starting pitch, fades out during the count-in, and is gone from bar 1. During the take the lane under the staff belongs to feedback only. On results, colour shows only on the clean score, never on the marked score. Feedback always wins.
- **Seven colours, one for each letter name**, in the classroom rainbow order (C red, D orange, E yellow, F green, G blue, A purple, B pink), retuned by the designer for our paper and themes. Sharps and flats take their letter's colour, and the chip says "F♯" or "B♭". No colour for the octave.
- **Two ways to colour, one setting.** By note name (fixed: C is always red) is the default for instruments. By scale degree (movable: the tonic is always red, with do, re, mi on the chip) is the default for voices. **Owner's choice** for both defaults.
- **On at difficulties 1 to 3, off above, unless the player turns it on.** It does not make a try practice, because nothing coloured is on screen while you play.
- **Never colour alone.** Every chip carries its letter or syllable. A "Letters only" setting gives the same help with no colour.

Recommended option: **B** (ink noteheads, a band of coloured letter chips in the read-through). Options A and C are below for the owner to compare.

## 1. What the colour means

### Decision

The colour follows **one written note name**, and the player picks what the name is:

| Setting | What is coloured | Chip shows | Default for |
|---|---|---|---|
| By note name | The written letter. C is always the C colour, in every key and octave. | `C`, `F♯`, `B♭` | Instruments (all of them, transposing included) |
| By scale degree | The note's degree in the key of the piece. The tonic is always the first colour. | `do`, `fi`, `te` (with the letter in the accessible name) | Voices |

**Owner's choice:** whether voices default to scale degree (my recommendation) or to note name. Both settings are always available to everyone.

### Why

- **For an instrumentalist reading at sight,** the job is "this line or space is this note, so this fingering". The letter is stable across keys and matches the fingering chart, the keyboard and every classroom colour system the player may have met. Colour by note name helps that link and nothing else.
- **For a singer,** the letter does not help pitch the note. Unless you have perfect pitch, "this is an E" says nothing about how it sounds. What a singer needs is where the note sits in the key: home, the fifth, the leading note. That is what movable do teaches, and colour by degree makes it visible: the tonic is always the same colour, so the singer learns to see "home" on any line. The owner is a baritone at difficulty 1, so this is the setting most likely to help him.
- **It also matches the app.** The pedagogy tips already use movable-do chromatic syllables (`docs/spec/02-pedagogy-and-grading.md`, VO04: "Sharps raise (fi, di, si), flats lower (te, le, me)"). Degree mode is do-based in minor as well (the tonic of A minor is do, its third is me), for the same reason. La-based minor is not offered.
- **In C major the two settings agree** (C is the tonic and the first colour), so a player who moves from one to the other has a shared starting point.

### Transposing instruments

- By note name colours the **written** note. A B♭ clarinet reading a written D sees the D colour, even though it sounds C. The player reads and fingers the written note, and the app already shows written ranges for transposing instruments (`05-ux-spec.md` §7).
- By scale degree is the same in written and sounding pitch, because the key transposes with the note. A clarinettist in written G major and a flautist in concert F major both see the tonic in the first colour.
- Tenor voice reads treble clef an octave down. The letter is the same, so nothing changes.

### Rules that follow from this

- **Degree colour needs a key.** Where there is no key (Match a note, the range picture in the picker), degree mode shows no colour, only the plain note name. It never guesses a key.
- **The key is the key of the excerpt** as the app already knows it. Chromatic notes and short tonicisations keep the home key's degrees, with raised or lowered syllables (fi, si, te, me, le). The app does not re-analyse modulations; the excerpts at difficulties 1 to 3 rarely have any.

## 2. Palette

### Decision

Our own seven colours, one for each letter (or each degree), **in the classroom order** so that anyone who learned with coloured tubes or bells sees the same rainbow running up from C:

| Letter (degree in C) | Colour family | Classroom match (from `01-research.md`, unverified) |
|---|---|---|
| C (do) | red, leaning crimson | red |
| D (re) | orange | orange |
| E (mi) | yellow, deepened to ochre on light paper | yellow |
| F (fa) | yellow-green | light green |
| G (sol) | blue | dark green or teal in the brief, blue in one snippet; we choose blue |
| A (la) | purple | purple |
| B (ti) | pink, leaning magenta | pink |

The designer sets the hex values, a light set and a dark set, and a high-contrast set. Section 5 gives the constraints. I am not proposing the approximate Chroma-Notes hex values from the research, for three reasons:

1. **They are unverified,** and the names are trademarks. We say "classroom colours" in help text, never "Boomwhackers" or "Chroma-Notes", and we do not claim to match any product. The research also lists patents on colour-notation schemes. Before release, someone who knows the law should check the plain rainbow-from-C order. I expect it is common practice, but that is not a legal view.
2. **Twelve colours cannot be told apart reliably,** on a phone, at a metre, by the 1 in 12 men with a colour vision deficiency. Seven are hard enough. Colouring by letter needs only seven.
3. **The bright, saturated product colours do not work on our paper,** in either theme, at the contrast the app already promises.

### Sharps, flats and enharmonics

- **The colour follows the spelling, not the sound.** F♯ is the F colour with "F♯" on the chip. G♭ is the G colour with "G♭". The sight-reader reads F♯ on the F line, so the F colour is right; the accidental is the second cue, on the chip.
- This is a deliberate break from the twelve-colour classroom sets, where F♯ and G♭ share a blend colour. Our reason: the app teaches reading the page, and on the page they are different notes.
- **Key signature accidentals count.** In F major every B shows as "B♭" in the B colour. This is exactly what the read-through's "Accidentals" row asks the player to spot, so the band supports it.
- Degree mode works the same way: raised and lowered degrees keep their degree's colour, and the syllable changes (fa to fi, ti to te).

### Octaves

- **Not shown by colour.** The staff already shows the octave, and changing lightness by octave would break the contrast rules in section 5. The research also says the pitch-height-to-brightness link is a weak tendency, not a convention anyone has learned.
- **Shown in text where the octave matters:** the starting-pitch card ("First note: F3"), Match a note ("E3"), the picker's note pills ("C3"). The chips in the band show the letter only.
- Figurenotes uses shape for octave. We do not need a second shape system: the staff is the shape system we are teaching.

## 3. Where colour appears, and where it must not

The rule: **colour and feedback are never on the same score at the same time.** Where marks can appear, colour is off. Where colour is on, nothing is being judged.

| Place | Colour? | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Read-through | **Yes, the main place** | A band of chips under each system, one chip under each notehead, aligned to its x. Noteheads stay ink. A "Colour key" link under the score opens the legend (section 5). |
| Starting-pitch card (voices) | Yes, on the card only | "First note: F3" with a chip (degree mode: "First note: F3, do"). The first note keeps its brass ring, because brass means "now". The band stays visible while the score is dimmed. |
| Count-in | Fades out | The band fades from 100% to 0 over the first count-in bar. During the last bar ("Come in after 4") there is no colour anywhere. Reduced motion: the band disappears at the first click. Instruments, which skip the starting pitch, fade the same way. |
| The take | **Never** | The lane under the staff is feedback only: the green dot, the red triangles, the ✕ and the ○. Noteheads are ink, red or ringed, as §7 of the design spec says. |
| Results, marked score ("My marks") | **Never** | Green, red and violet own this view. |
| Results, clean score | Yes, if the setting is on | The same band as the read-through. Switching to "My marks" hides it at once. Listen plays over the clean score with the band and the brass bar wash. |
| History: try detail | As results | Clean score only. |
| History: list | No | Nothing to colour. |
| Match a note | Yes, note-name mode only | A chip beside the 88 px target name ("E3"). The range chips at the bottom ("C3 E3 G3 C4") each get a colour swatch. The needle, the in-tune band and "You hit it" stay ink and green exactly as now. The "You: E3, 34 cents low" line has no colour, because it reports what you sang, not what was written. |
| Picker: range picture | Note pills only | A 10 px swatch inside each pill, before the name. The served pills stay ink-filled and the full pills stay outlined, so the served-and-full meaning is not touched. No colour on the four whole notes on the staff. |
| Picker: keyboard strip | No | The keys already carry the full and served range tones, and the brackets carry the range. Twelve coloured keys would hide that. The coloured keyboard lives in the legend instead. |
| Key grid and key chips | No | In degree mode the tonic would always be red, which tells you nothing. In note-name mode it would look like a "key colour" system (Scriabin), which we are not offering. |
| Calibration, Settings rows, toasts | No | |

### Why the band, not the noteheads

- §7 of the design spec uses the notehead for the verdict (vermilion for a slip, the ring for a miss). A coloured notehead would compete with that, and a red C would read as a mistake.
- The band sits in **the lane slot** (2.2 staff spaces under the staff), which is empty until bar 1. So the read-through, when colour is on, uses the take's layout with the lane, and the music does not move when Start is pressed. This is the rule from round 2: the score never moves between the reference, the count-in and bar 1. When colour is off, the read-through keeps today's layout (no lane).
- At take sizes a notehead is about 19 px wide (design spec §7). A letter inside it would be about 9 px, too small to read at a metre. A chip under it can be as large as the spacing allows.

### Coexisting with green, red and purple

Because the two never share a view, a red C chip cannot be read as a mistake during a try. Two extra guards:

- **Chip shape is not a feedback shape.** A chip is a rounded rectangle with a letter. No feedback mark is a rectangle or contains a letter. The correct dot, the triangles, ✕, ○ and + stay unique.
- **The palette avoids the exact feedback hues.** C leans crimson, away from vermilion `#C23A12`. F is yellow-green, away from teal-green `#1F7A5C`. A is a redder purple than ghost violet `#6C3FC4`. E and D stay clear of brass `#94661A`, which is "now". Section 5 gives the test.

## 4. Defaults, difficulty and ranked tries

### Setting

Settings › Display, one row with a segmented control, and a second row under it:

- **Note help:** Off · Letters · Letters and colours
- **Show it:** At difficulties 1 to 3 · Always
- **Colour by** (only when "Letters and colours" is chosen): Note name · Scale degree

The read-through also gets a quiet toggle beside "Colour key", "Hide note help" / "Show note help", which changes this try only. That is the on-demand path for a player above difficulty 3 who has turned it off, and for a player who wants to test himself once.

### Default

- **New players:** Letters and colours, at difficulties 1 to 3. The colour by setting follows the instrument (section 1).
- **Existing players, the owner included:** the same. He asked for it, and he is at difficulty 1.
- **From difficulty 4,** the band does not show unless "Always" is chosen or the player shows it for a try. The difficulty line on Practice says so once, the first time a player reaches difficulty 4 with note help on: "From difficulty 4 the music has no note help. You can turn it back on in Settings › Display."

**Owner's choice:** whether the cut-off is after difficulty 3 (my recommendation, the same line as the calmer live marks) or after difficulty 2.

### Why it fades

The research reports one study (Rogers 1991, cited, not read) in which children taught with colour-coded notation read plain notation worse. The app's job is plain notation. So colour is a ramp that runs out: present in the read-through at the lowest difficulties, gone during every take, gone from the read-through above difficulty 3 by default. A player always plays from ink. That is the whole point of fading at the count-in.

### Ranked tries

**Note help does not make a try practice.** Compare Listen first, which does (PLAN ruling 59): listening gives you the sound of the answer, and you can sing it back from memory. Note help gives you the names of the written notes, which a reader can work out from the page. It is gone before bar 1, so you play from the same ink as everyone else. It is a reading aid in the read-through, like the "Look before you play" list, which does not affect ranked status either.

If the owner prefers option A (colour on the noteheads during the take), that changes: colour during the take is help with the answer, and those tries must count as practice with a reason such as `note-colours`, worded on results as "Note colours were on, so this one is practice."

## 5. Accessibility

### Never colour alone

- Every chip has its letter (with ♯ or ♭) or its syllable. The colour is a second cue on top of the text, never the only one.
- **Letters only** gives the full benefit with no colour, for anyone who finds the colours noisy or cannot tell them apart.
- In the legend, each colour is named in words ("C: red").

### Contrast targets for the designer

- **Chip text on chip fill: at least 4.5:1**, in light, dark and high contrast. The letter is the information, so it is held to the text standard.
- **Chip edge against paper: at least 3:1.** Pale fills (E yellow, F yellow-green, B pink on light paper) need a 1 px edge in a darker shade of the same hue, or the fill deepened, to get there.
- **Dark paper (`#24221F`) gets its own set:** fills a step lighter and less saturated, dark letter (`#1A1917`) inside. Do not reuse the light fills.
- **High contrast (`[data-contrast="high"]`):** a 1.5 px `ink` edge on every chip, the letter in 600 weight, fills unchanged.
- **Forced colours:** chips draw as system text in a system-coloured outline. The letter carries everything.
- Add every chip pair to the contrast test that covers every token pair today.

### Colour vision

- Under the Machado simulations already used in the spec (protanopia, deuteranopia, tritanopia), the designer should report ΔE between neighbouring letters (C and D, F and G, and so on) and between each chip and the feedback colours. There is no pass mark for neighbours, because the letter carries the meaning; the numbers tell us which pairs need more lightness difference.
- Red against green (C and F) is the known risk. Give C and F clearly different lightness, so they separate in greyscale too.

### Screen readers

- The band is `aria-hidden`. The read-through's accessible description gains one line per system when note help is on, such as "Bar 1: F, G, A, B flat" (degree mode: "do, re, mi, fa"). Off by default for screen-reader users, who get note names on request with the same setting.

### Legend

- Settings › Display shows one octave of keyboard, C to B, with each white key's chip under it (degree mode: degrees 1 to 7 of C major, with the line "In other keys the colours move with the key: do is always red.").
- The read-through's "Colour key" opens a popover with the seven chips in a row, and the same one-line explanation.
- The picker caption gets nothing new. The swatches on the pills explain themselves next to the note name.

### Motion

- The fade during the count-in is 4 seconds at ♩ = 60, opacity only. Under reduced motion it is a cut at the first click.

## 6. Three options for the owner

| | A: Coloured noteheads | B: Ink noteheads, coloured letter band (recommended) | C: Colour on demand only |
|---|---|---|---|
| Read-through | Every notehead in its colour | Chips with letters in the lane slot, noteheads ink | Ink only. "Show note help" button reveals the band while pressed or toggled |
| Starting pitch, count-in | Colour stays | Card chip; band fades in the first count-in bar | Ink only |
| Take | Colour stays, feedback draws over it | Nothing coloured | Nothing coloured |
| Results | Clean score coloured | Clean score shows the band | Ink only |
| Match a note, picker pills | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Ranked | Must count as practice | Unchanged | Unchanged |
| Looks like | A children's colour score | A score with a study aid under it | Today's app |
| Main risk | A red C reads as a mistake; the vermilion slip notehead is lost; becomes a crutch | Chips crowd in dense rhythms; one more thing in the read-through | Few people find it; little benefit |

### Recommendation

**B, with C's button for difficulties 4 and above.** It gives the owner what he asked for on the screens where he learns the notes, keeps every feedback rule as it is, never puts colour in front of him while he is being marked, and leaves ranked tries alone. A is the most familiar from classrooms, but it breaks the notehead feedback that took two rounds to get right, and it makes colour part of the take, which is the crutch the research warns about.

**Open question for the designer:** chip size and what happens when notes are close. At difficulties 1 to 3 the rhythms are simple and full chips should fit. For "Always" at higher difficulties, where sixteenths can sit closer than a chip is wide, I would let chips collapse to a 0.6 staff-space colour dot with no letter, and keep the letter on every first note of a beat. The designer should draw this and tell me if it reads. Also check the lane slot against low notes with several ledger lines (a baritone's G2 in bass clef), as the lane glyphs already have to.

### What I would test

With the owner first, then about five learners at difficulties 1 to 3 (singers and instrumentalists, at least one man with a colour vision deficiency). The app has no server, so these are sessions run in person or over a call.

1. **Five-second meaning test.** Show the read-through with the band, then a marked result. Ask "what does the red mean here?" Pass: nobody reads a C chip as a mistake, or an F chip as correct.
2. **Does it help the try?** Alternate tries with note help on and off at the same difficulty, about ten each. Compare scores and the pitch-mistake count. We expect a small gain with help on, at difficulties 1 and 2.
3. **Does it hurt plain reading?** After two weeks with help on, run five tries at difficulty 4 (no help). Compare with the player's tries before. Pass: no drop.
4. **Note name or scale degree, for singers.** The owner tries each for a few days. Ask which made it easier to find the first note and to hear the leaps. This settles the voice default.
5. **The fade.** Ask whether the band fading during the count-in felt like losing something or like a clear "now read". If it is unsettling, try cutting it at the start of the last count-in bar instead.
6. **Colour vision.** The CVD tester names the notes from chips only, then from colour only (letters hidden in a test build). We expect letters to carry it; colour-only results tell us which pairs to fix.
7. **Legibility at a metre.** Chips in light and dark themes, on a laptop on a music stand. Letters must be readable where the notes are.
